A camera that keeps only
what surprised it.

A background model predicts every frame. What it predicts is thrown away; what it fails to predict is kept, with a sparse keyframe spine so the gaps stay reconstructible. An empty corridor costs almost nothing per hour. The moment something happens costs everything — which is the point.

The result on a controlled clip

A synthetic corridor: 144 frames, empty except for one person crossing at frames 54–108. The tool detects exactly that window, peaking at frame 54 — the moment of entry — and stores 57 of 144 frames for a 60.4 % byte saving.

The live counter above runs the same arithmetic with browser JPEG at quality 55 and reads a point or two lower on savings; the Python figure is the reference, the on-page number is what the browser actually encoded.

That saving is modest because the event is 38 % of this clip. On a real camera the interesting fraction is closer to 1 %, so the same arithmetic lands near 99 % — which is the entire economic argument. The demo is deliberately event-dense so the mechanism is visible.

The trap I fell into first

My initial metric was the mean absolute difference over the whole frame. A person crossing a 320×180 corridor scored 0.017 — the wall outvoted them — so the threshold sat above the peak and the tool reported 97.9 % saved while storing nothing. A storage system that stores nothing looks perfect by every metric except the one that matters. The fix is a floor-subtracted change mass plus an adaptive threshold at three times the median.

What this is not

Not a compression codec — it does not reconstruct skipped frames from their neighbours, it just keeps a keyframe spine. Not robust to lighting changes: a light switching on is genuine surprise and will be stored (correctly), but a slow sunset will keep the model confused until it adapts. And not a substitute for retention policy — "store only surprise" still needs a rule for when surprise is irrelevant.
